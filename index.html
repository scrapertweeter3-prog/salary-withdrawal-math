A raise is a withdrawal in disguise

The 4% rule prices spending in capital: every $1 of yearly spending is $25 of portfolio set aside to fund it. Turn the lens around and income prices the same way. A recurring $1,000 a year is $25,000 of capital doing the identical job, so every salary line on an offer letter is a withdrawal from an invisible portfolio.

A $10,000 raise is $250,000 of equivalent corpus before tax. At a 24% marginal rate the actual check is $7,600, which replaces $190,000 of portfolio. The $2,400 withholding takes is $60,000 of corpus that never shows up in an offer comparison, yet two offers that differ by exactly that withholding are not the same offer once the capital math runs.

Commutes carry the same tag. Forty miles a day across 250 working days is 10,000 miles; at a round 30 cents a mile that is $3,000, and $75 monthly parking adds $900. A $3,900 commute is a $97,500 portfolio at 25x, and the parking alone is $22,500. An offer $4,000 higher on paper with the longer drive nets about $100 a year once the drive is priced, which is two offers a rounding error apart wearing a $100,000 costume.

The recurring lines price faster than the checks. A $120 monthly health premium difference is $1,440 a year, or $36,000 of corpus. Ledger the recurring numbers first and the headline salary is the last thing worth reading.
